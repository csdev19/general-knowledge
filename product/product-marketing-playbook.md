# Product Marketing Playbook

**Lead with the meaningful change, then make it credible.** A product page should help the right person recognize their situation, understand the progress the product enables, see how it works, believe the claim, and take an appropriate next step.

This is an execution playbook, not a collection of copywriting rules. Each stage produces an artifact that constrains the next one. Do not write the landing page before the evidence, positioning, and claim boundaries exist.

## The core model

Use this causal chain:

```text
Circumstance -> problem -> desired progress -> product category
-> mechanism -> differentiated value -> proof -> action
```

The public page should live mainly in the desired progress and differentiated value. Category, mechanism, and proof make that promise understandable and believable. Features explain the mechanism; they are not automatically evidence.

The editing question is:

> Are we listing what we built, or showing why the right person will be glad it exists?

If a statement only describes the product, move through this ladder:

```text
Feature -> capability -> operational effect -> human or team benefit -> outcome
```

Stop at the highest layer the available evidence can support.

## The deliverables

Apply the playbook through a sequence of small specifications. Each one answers a different question and can be revised without rewriting everything downstream.

| # | Specification | Question answered | Output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Evidence and claim baseline | What do we know, infer, and still need to prove? | Source ledger and claim register |
| 2 | Customer progress | In what circumstance does someone seek change? | Job card and before-state narrative |
| 3 | Alternatives | What happens if the product does not exist? | Competitive-alternatives table |
| 4 | Positioning | For whom are we unusually valuable, and in what category? | Positioning canvas |
| 5 | Message architecture | Which promise leads, and what supports it? | Message hierarchy |
| 6 | Proof strategy | What will make each important claim believable? | Proof plan |
| 7 | Conversion journey | What must visitors understand before acting? | Page argument and CTA map |
| 8 | Visual demonstration | What should visitors see instead of merely read? | Visual evidence plan |
| 9 | Page specification | How do copy, visuals, and interactions form one argument? | Responsive page specification |
| 10 | Validation | What would show that the argument works or fails? | Test plan and findings log |

Do not collapse these into one copywriting exercise. A clever headline cannot repair weak positioning or missing evidence.

## 1. Evidence and claim baseline

### Inputs

- Customer interviews and observed workflows
- Sales, support, onboarding, and cancellation language
- Usage data and task-completion data
- Customer outcomes and case studies
- Competitor and status-quo alternatives
- Product capabilities and limitations
- Traffic source, buyer stage, and intended action

### Actions

1. Record each useful statement as **observed**, **measured**, **reported**, **inferred**, or **aspirational**.
2. Preserve the customer's words for the problem, trigger, anxiety, and desired result.
3. Separate one customer's result from a representative result.
4. Add an owner and review date to every objective or comparative claim.
5. Record important unknowns rather than smoothing them into copy.

### Claim register

```text
Claim:
Claim type: factual | comparative | quantitative | testimonial | aspirational
Audience:
Evidence:
Evidence status: observed | measured | reported | inferred | missing
Representative or exceptional:
Required qualification:
Owner:
Review date:
Approved contexts:
```

### Stop condition

The team can distinguish facts from hypotheses, and no quantitative or comparative claim is scheduled for publication without a reasonable basis.

## 2. Customer progress

A useful audience is not only a demographic or job title. It is a person in a recognizable circumstance trying to make progress.

### Job card

```text
Actor:
Triggering circumstance:
Current workflow:
What is blocked, slow, risky, confusing, or unpleasant:
Desired progress:
Functional stakes:
Emotional stakes:
Social or team stakes:
Anxieties about changing:
Success criteria:
```

### Before-state narrative

Write the existing sequence without mentioning the product:

```text
Trigger -> current action -> dependency or friction -> workaround
-> delay, risk, or ownership burden -> eventual result
```

Do not invent pain because it makes a stronger story. If the problem is occasional or low-cost, say so; that may mean the segment is not a good fit.

### Stop condition

A target customer can read the circumstance and say, "That is what happens to us," before seeing the proposed solution.

## 3. Competitive alternatives

The primary competitor may be waiting, manual work, an internal tool, a spreadsheet, another department, or doing nothing. Named products are only relevant when buyers actually consider them.

| Alternative | Why people choose it | Full cost | Where it wins | Where it fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Status quo | | | | |
| Build internally | | | | |
| Manual workaround | | | | |
| Named product | | | | |
| No decision | | | | |

For internal tools, count the whole ownership burden: design, implementation, hosting, maintenance, documentation, onboarding, troubleshooting, and drift. Do not compare only against the cost of version one.

### Stop condition

The alternatives reflect observed buyer behavior, not a list of theoretically adjacent competitors.

## 4. Positioning

Positioning is the set of decisions that gives the product useful context. It is not a tagline.

Work in this order:

1. Actual competitive alternatives
2. Capabilities those alternatives do not provide
3. Value those capabilities create
4. Customers who care disproportionately about that value
5. A market category that makes the value easier to understand

### Positioning canvas

```text
Best-fit customer and circumstance:
Actual alternatives:
Differentiated capabilities:
Operational value:
Higher-level value:
Market category:
Category expectations we satisfy:
Category expectations we intentionally reject:
Evidence available:
```

Prefer an established category when it gives buyers accurate expectations. Creating a new category adds an education problem and needs a concrete reason and enough distribution to support it.

### Compression test

After the positioning is decided, compress it into one sentence:

```text
For <best-fit customer> facing <circumstance>, <product> is a <category>
that <meaningful change> through <differentiated mechanism>.
```

This sentence tests clarity. It is not necessarily public copy.

### Stop condition

The team can explain why this audience should choose the product over what they already do, without relying on generic claims such as "easy," "powerful," or "AI-powered."

## 5. Outcome ladder and message architecture

For every important capability, ask "so what?" until the answer reaches a meaningful operational or human change.

| Layer | Question | Example shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feature | What exists? | Imports an agreed contract |
| Capability | What can the user do? | Generate a working substitute from that contract |
| Operational effect | What changes in the workflow? | A dependent task can begin before the real service exists |
| Team benefit | Why does that matter? | Two teams can work in parallel instead of serially |
| Outcome | What improves if the mechanism holds? | Less blocked delivery time |

The ladder is causal, not decorative. If a feature does not reliably create the claimed outcome, weaken the claim or identify the missing condition.

### Message hierarchy

```text
Primary promise:
Best-fit audience and circumstance:
Product category:
Mechanism in one sentence:
Supporting outcome 1:
Supporting outcome 2:
Supporting outcome 3:
Proof for each outcome:
Main objection:
Response to the objection:
Capabilities that support the argument:
Primary action:
Lower-commitment action:
```

Use one primary promise. Supporting messages can explain distinct benefits, but they must reinforce rather than compete with it.

### Stop condition

Every prominent benefit has a plausible mechanism and planned proof; every prominent capability supports a benefit the target audience cares about.

## 6. Proof strategy

Before/after framing explains a transformation; it does not prove one. Match the proof to the claim.

| Claim | Useful proof |
| --- | --- |
| The workflow is shorter | Side-by-side task flow or timed demonstration |
| Setup is fast | Representative task-completion measurement |
| The product handles a scenario | Live or recorded product demonstration |
| A technical property holds | Documentation, test result, architecture explanation, or reproducible check |
| Customers get an outcome | Representative customer evidence with context |
| The product is better than an alternative | Fair comparative test with disclosed conditions |

Proof should be close to the claim it supports. Logos, generic testimonials, and screenshots without context do not automatically prove the primary promise.

### Stop condition

Each objective claim has prior substantiation, and exceptional results are not presented as typical.

## 7. Page argument

A landing page is a sequence of questions answered in the order this visitor needs them answered. A useful default is:

1. **Orientation:** What is this, who is it for, and what meaningful change does it promise?
2. **Recognition:** Is this my circumstance or problem?
3. **Progress:** What changes from before to after?
4. **Mechanism:** How does the product create that change?
5. **Proof:** Why should I believe it?
6. **Fit:** Which use cases, constraints, and integrations matter to me?
7. **Depth:** Which capabilities make the promise possible?
8. **Trust:** What objections, limitations, security, or compatibility questions remain?
9. **Action:** What should I do next, and what will happen?

This is a default argument, not a mandatory section order. Category-aware or high-intent visitors may need compatibility, pricing, or technical detail much earlier.

### Hero comprehension test

After a brief exposure, a target visitor should be able to answer:

1. What kind of product is this?
2. Is it for someone in my situation?
3. What meaningful change does it promise?
4. How does it broadly create that change?
5. Why should I continue?
6. What happens if I take the primary action?

Do not sacrifice category clarity for a clever outcome line. Outcome-first does not mean outcome-only.

### One section, one persuasive job

Give every section one job, such as establish recognition, explain a mechanism, prove a claim, resolve an objection, or prompt action. A section may contain several related facts when they are all needed to complete that job.

### Stop condition

Removing or moving a section has an explainable effect on the argument. No section exists only because similar websites have one.

## 8. Visual evidence

Every visual must answer a visitor question:

- What does using this feel like?
- What changes in the workflow?
- How quickly can I reach the result?
- What happens after this action?
- How does the product handle a realistic case?
- What makes the mechanism different?

Prefer state changes and short workflows over generic dashboards:

```text
Before -> product mechanism -> after
```

Annotate the relevant details. Pair visuals with concise text and accessible alternatives; a screenshot alone rarely proves an outcome.

### Before/after diagrams

A good before/after diagram shows:

- The same actor and goal on both sides
- The dependency or friction that disappears
- The product mechanism responsible for the change
- Any condition required for the transition to work
- The final handoff, not only the temporary happy state

The last point matters for products that temporarily replace another system. Showing how users return to the real system prevents "this only moves integration pain later" from remaining unanswered.

### Stop condition

Each visual has a named question, a factual basis, and a reason to be more effective than text alone.

## 9. Progressive disclosure and technical depth

Organize information in layers:

1. Why should I care?
2. How does this help in my situation?
3. How does it work?
4. What are the technical details?

Keep category, audience, the primary mechanism, meaningful limitations, and proof visible. Defer secondary implementation details, exhaustive feature lists, and specialist configuration.

Technical differentiation often works best as proof of a human promise:

```text
Human promise -> technical mechanism -> verifiable detail
```

### Stop condition

New visitors can understand the offer without technical overload, while evaluators can find the detail required to trust and adopt it.

## 10. Calls to action

A CTA should communicate:

- The action
- What happens next
- The value or expectation attached to it

Match commitment to readiness. A visitor who has not yet seen the product may need a demo or example before signup; a high-intent visitor may want to start immediately.

Do not invent many clever CTAs that fragment the conversion path. Use one primary action and, when needed, one lower-commitment alternative.

### Stop condition

The primary CTA is the logical next step in the page's argument and its result is predictable.

## 11. Writing and brand

Write for scanning:

- Put the conclusion first.
- Use meaningful headings rather than teaser headings.
- Keep one idea per paragraph.
- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs over claims such as "seamless" or "revolutionary."
- Use the customer's vocabulary when it is accurate.
- Remove every sentence that repeats the heading without adding mechanism, evidence, or consequence.

Brand personality should make the product memorable without making it harder to understand. "Human, playful, and technically credible" can be a useful product-family direction, but it is not universal guidance. Validate tone against the audience, category, purchase risk, and context.

### Stop condition

The page remains clear without its visual styling, and its personality does not hide meaning or inflate claims.

## 12. Validation and maintenance

Validate the argument in increasing order of cost:

1. **Comprehension:** Can target visitors explain the category, audience, promise, and mechanism?
2. **Recognition:** Do they recognize the problem in their own language?
3. **Relevance:** Do they care enough about the promised change?
4. **Differentiation:** Is the product preferable to their actual alternative?
5. **Credibility:** Which claims do they doubt, and what proof would change that?
6. **Usability:** Can they find the information and complete the intended action?
7. **Behavior:** Does the page improve qualified actions, not only clicks?

Review the playbook outputs when:

- The best-fit audience changes
- Buyers start using a new alternative
- A central capability or limitation changes
- Proof expires or is contradicted
- The category creates inaccurate expectations
- Validation repeatedly shows the message is misunderstood

## Illustrative example: remove the dependency, not merely the task

A public engineering example described a frontend team blocked by unfinished APIs. The visible artifact was a Node.js mock server. The mechanism was development against an agreed API contract. The meaningful change was structural: frontend and backend work could proceed in parallel instead of serially.

The marketing ladder is:

```text
Mock server
-> usable responses before the real API exists
-> frontend work can begin without waiting
-> teams work in parallel
-> less blocked delivery time
```

The example contributes four reusable lessons:

1. **Look for workflow topology changes.** Serial work becoming parallel can be more valuable than making one task faster.
2. **Treat internal ownership as an alternative.** "Build it ourselves" includes maintenance, hosting, onboarding, and drift, not only initial implementation.
3. **State the conditions.** Reduced integration work depends on a shared contract and control of drift between the substitute and the real implementation.
4. **Show the handoff.** The story is incomplete until it explains how work moves from the substitute to the real system.

The reported result of one team working two or three sprints ahead is evidence for that team's implementation, not a general benchmark or a claim another product can inherit.

## Anti-patterns

- Product -> features -> more features -> pricing
- Outcome language so vague that the visitor cannot identify the product category
- A feature grid presented as proof of customer outcomes
- Generic dashboard screenshots with no question to answer
- "Easy" or "fast" without a representative task or comparison
- A before/after story that hides prerequisites or moves pain to a later step
- Competitor research that ignores waiting, internal tools, and no decision
- Customer quotes presented without enough context to understand the result
- Technical detail hidden when it is essential to trust, compatibility, or risk
- Copy written before the team agrees on audience, alternatives, and evidence

## Page review checklist

### Positioning

- [ ] The audience and triggering circumstance are specific.
- [ ] The actual alternatives include the status quo and internal workarounds.
- [ ] The category creates accurate expectations.
- [ ] Differentiation is tied to a value the audience cares about.

### Message

- [ ] One primary promise leads the page.
- [ ] The hero communicates category, audience, outcome, mechanism, and action.
- [ ] Each section has one persuasive job.
- [ ] Features support the promise instead of replacing it.
- [ ] Technical language is translated into operational or human value.

### Proof

- [ ] Every objective or comparative claim has prior support.
- [ ] Reported and exceptional outcomes are labelled honestly.
- [ ] Before/after framing does not pretend to establish causality.
- [ ] Important prerequisites and limitations are visible.

### Visuals and action

- [ ] Every visual answers a named visitor question.
- [ ] Product visuals show a workflow, state change, result, or differentiator.
- [ ] The primary CTA follows logically from the argument.
- [ ] Visitors know what will happen after acting.

### Validation

- [ ] Target visitors can restate the offer accurately.
- [ ] Doubts and objections are recorded rather than guessed away.
- [ ] Success measures qualified action, not only engagement.
- [ ] Claims and market observations have owners and review dates.
